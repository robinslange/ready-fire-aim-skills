# Ready, Fire, Aim

From Michael Masterson, *Ready, Fire, Aim* (2008), ch 11-14.

**Core principle:** you cannot know whether an idea is good until someone tries to buy it. Spend a short, disciplined "ready", fire the cheapest test that replicates the real sale, and spend the money on perfection only after the market says yes. This is not "fire at will": the gun goes to the shoulder before it goes off.

Not for safety-critical or high-fixed-cost builds (payments correctness, data integrity, anything where a flawed v1 harms someone). There "good" must mean correct; ready-fire-aim still governs the *business* bet around it.

## Scale for a solo business

Masterson's figures are from $10M publishers. For one person:
- **Stop-loss in hours and weeks**, not only money: "10 asks and 2 paid pilots by <date>, or it's dead".
- **The test competes with paid work.** On a short runway, price the test in hours you could have billed. If the test doesn't fit in those hours, choose a smaller test.
- **Experienced gut** = the nearest person who has sold to this buyer: an existing client in the field, a vendor already selling to them, a peer founder. If there is nobody, that's a finding. Prefer an idea closer to buyers you already reach.
- **Hit rates** (1 good idea in 2-3) argue for several *small* tests, not one big one.

## Ready: hours, not weeks

Answer the seven questions. Any "no" means fix it or don't fire.

| # | Question | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is it a good idea? | One big customer benefit, so easy to say that the buyer instantly sees themselves getting it. "Wouldn't it be cool if" fails. |
| 2 | Does it feel like it will work? | Ask the most experienced person you can reach. Weigh their gut over your spreadsheet. |
| 3 | Are the targets realistic? | Set a minimum sales number. Then **double the cost, halve the returns**. Still profitable? Go. Marginal? Next idea. |
| 4 | Can I afford to *test* it? | Price the test, not the launch. |
| 5 | Do I know the basic tasks? | A task list that takes a few hours, with who does each. |
| 6 | Do I have the people? | A champion who believes in it, has authority, and has done this before. No champion = postpone. |
| 7 | Plan B? | **Stop-loss set before firing**: the number (money, weeks, responses) at which you stop. Write it down. |

Output of Ready: a **1-4 page proposal written within 24 hours**: rough financials (with the overlooked costs), critical tasks, champion, dated timeline, stop-loss. If planning runs to weeks, it is procrastination, because what matters most about a market can't be seen from outside it.

## Fire: the smallest test that replicates the real sale

- The test must ask for the real commitment: money, a signed quote, a booked call. Surveys and "would you use this?" measure politeness.
- **Split compound uncertainty.** Test the riskiest, cheapest unknown first (will anyone pay for X at all?) before the expensive one (is our X good?).
- Prefer, in order: pre-sell before building; sell an existing or manual version with your angle on it; offer it free to existing customers for feedback; a dry test (take orders, refund, build only if it clears).
- Existing customers first. If it fails with people who already trust you, it fails with strangers. A targeted success is encouragement, not proof.
- "Good" is the bar, not perfect. Never ship something seriously flawed. Imperfections after that are profit opportunities.
- Two things that stop launches: perfectionism and "little chores first". If you hear "once X is in place", X is the chore.

## Aim: after the market answers

```
Did it sell (hit the minimum number before the stop-loss)?
  no  -> trash it. Do not tweak a non-seller; build something significantly different.
  yes -> improve it, best sellers first:
         one change at a time, reversible, watch the response;
         test every change against the ORIGINAL baseline, not the last version
         (small cuts compound into a visible quality loss).
```

Expect most tests to die: roughly 1 in 2-3 ideas is good and 1 in 5-10 is excellent. Speed of testing beats idea quality, so aim to run tests at least twice as fast as feels comfortable. Log every test (idea, test, number, result, decision) somewhere searchable, and check new ideas against it before firing.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Full architecture, pricing tiers and landing page before one buyer conversation | Ready = 24h proposal. Fire = one real ask. |
| Stop-loss decided after the money is spent | Write it in the proposal, before firing. Masterson's $250K cap became $350K. Caps slip, so enforce them. |
| Brand-new idea with no market signal | Evolution, not revolution: 80% familiar, 20% new. Every one of his >$500K losses was a revolution. |
| Rescuing a weak seller with polish | If it doesn't sell, trash it. If it sells, improve it. |
| Running the test on a channel or audience unlike the real one | The test must replicate what you want to achieve. |
